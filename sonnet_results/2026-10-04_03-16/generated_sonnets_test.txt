Those lips that Love's own hand did make
Breathed forth the sound that said "I hate"
To me that languished for her sake;
Such lips can the best poets form
To beauties such as I, in praise of yours,
Describe in their song, though seldom;
Such tongues as these do so much honor
To each other's love and thoughts, which they so plainly
Hear in other tongues expressing.
  Such tongues have sweet voices that even my fair cheek
  Loves and keeps in her love's sweetmost ear;
  But that tongue, that of most proud niggard,
  From time immemorial stands in my best book.

Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
With thine own self the ground whereon thou standest?
If I am speaking of myself, thou hast my self too,
And this tender friend with me besets injury.
So I make war on myself and myself on thee,
Thou wilt not hold me in debt by fair fight,
But by strangling myself and my friend's life,
  To pay back for that wrong I did thee in youth.

2

My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
Returning home to the place whence it was taken,
Returning not to that which he left behind,
Returning not to his love, his freedom;
Suffering this, suffering that, and he dies.
If I love not, I cannot live,
Unless I die; and that I die
Is not my love's disgrace, it is my death.
  If I love, die; and that I live,
  He that loved me loves me not more.

3

O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
That leads me away from the truth?
O, no eyes, but mine own thought have filled
These parts with my invention, that they should see
My other base parts where the eyes are;
And yet, seeing these, I esteem them less good
Than when they are made in my head.
What reasons did my frame require
To have eyes in a glass, which do not see?
  O, they were a glass for their owner's sight,
  To make the argument, that the glass was made for my sake.

4

Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot thee,
And remember thee in every moan of my love?
O, no, gentle friend; but in all my thoughts do I love thee
As thyself; not to say that I trust in thee,
But that I believe in thee more than in myself.
For thou art the substance of mine own love,
Which I, the common owner, in my substance hold,
And in myself thy substance is added.
  But as soon as I remember thee in a sigh,
  I find myself thinking of thee again.  This is called love in my heart.

5

O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
To thrust my true love behind,
To constrain thy love's course with argument,
And waste thy glory by my love's abuse!
Such a course hath thou already committed
And taught me to pervert my truth,
To tell lies and maintain thy glory,
To basely mock thy true self, and to scorn thy love
And abuse him with his own fault.
  All this thou hast done with mine eye,
  Which thou hast not yet broken open till now.

Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Be not proud of having got my love so far.
Love hath to be kept in self-control;
And if self-control be absent,
Self-love is the worst of enemies.
Love must be borne, though it be borne
As that which bears his eye, so is it borne
When his love leads it with eye to eye;
But in this I perceive his love is wrought.
Even so it is with his sweet child
Who in him looks for rest, and, being found,

In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn;
That thou didst not swear in thy nightingale,
But was new a lie born; that I was not then deceived,
But love's scythe had smitten him to the heart;
That thou hadst not second thoughts of me deceived,
But second thoughts then of thy art;
That thou hast first thought of me deceived,
And hadst first thought of me deceived in me.
  This I do deny of thee, I confess of thee
  For I know thee so well as a jewel doth shine,
  That when thou art in me thou art but mistress.

8

Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep
In brocade and brocade's fine hue;
The fair, pale Dian in the shade,
Barely perceiving his virtue she wakened;
And then, losing his wit, she fell
In the fair's pure love with him,
Into the cold fire, burn, and torture him.
  The maid's poor wakened, and now was there
  The poor poet, dreaming on his flame.

9

The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
Ran as knights in pursuit, devising means to win,
By mortal scythe, poisoned knife, or with earth,
Casting a mortal ambush on his pride.
Then must he be mocked, and most vile made strong,
By the rage of a married woman,
Or in his woe slayed his tomb with no tomb to tomb lie.
  But his sweet love's worth was so great,
  Then all honor and praise, so great, were alike in him.

10

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Haply told of pleasure, and yet know
The humor of many a frown.
O, the sweets which my love best confounds,
She confounds with muses of stone, birds
Who know not sweetness nor yield their dainty droops,
Who love no new-found sport, nor hope of new
As she with joys which I myself now do boast.
Now I love her, though I never saw her,
She loves me, though I have a better heart
Than that maiden maiden at her wedlock.
  To love is to be beloved to die,


11

Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
Of rare sense and learning, of wit and eloquence;
That I have a second, a false, a beauteous lam,
And for my love of his am assailed.
At first these besmeared loves did increase,
But now, when I am weary and desert,
They sing more eloquently and less sigh;
The true and false more true and false;
The better for their sweet love and the worse for mine;
  Then, to purge my bosom of this double plague,
  I like to drown both.
